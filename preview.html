A book of small better days

I Know, But I Can Do Nothing

But you can make it better.

by Venkata Sandeep Dhullipalla

We all have those moments. You know the right thing, and you feel you can do nothing about it. This book is about the minute after that moment: one minute of thought, one small decent move, and the day gets better for everyone in the room.

Chapter 1: The Pressured Coworker

The moment

Tuesday, just after ten in the morning. The light came in low across the conference table, and eight of us sat with our laptops open and our coffee going cold. Mara was presenting the release update. She had been up late on Sunday fixing a bug, and you could hear it in the careful way she spoke, as if she had rehearsed every question the night before and was still afraid of the one she had not.

Daniel, our delivery lead, had been pressed by the client that morning. It was on him before he said a word, in the way he held his pen, in the way he did not look up from his notes.

He cut in halfway through her second slide.

“Mara, why is the payment fix still not in? I asked for this on Friday.”

“The API from the other team only landed this morning,” she said. “I had the fix ready within an hour of that.”

“I don’t want reasons,” Daniel said. “I want it done.”

The room went quiet. Mara looked down at her notes and apologised for a delay that was not hers, and something in me went quiet with her.

Because I knew. The payment API had landed that morning, and Mara had turned the fix around within the hour. I knew Daniel knew it too. He had seen the same update I had. But his own boss had pressed him that morning, and pressure, once it is passed down, has to land somewhere. Mara was the nearest place.

And I thought the sentence I have thought a hundred times in rooms like that one. I know, but I can do nothing. If I speak, Daniel turns on me. It is not my fight. Mara can handle herself. Someone else will say something.

Nobody did. The silence in that room was the sound of eight people agreeing, without a word, to let one person carry what belonged to all of us.

The minute

Something made me wait one minute instead of letting the moment pass. In that minute, I stopped rehearsing my own excuses and asked myself what was actually true.

The delay was the dependency, not the work, and not Mara. That was the truth, and it was a small truth, the kind that fits in one sentence.

And then I asked what would help her, not in general, but right now, in this minute, in this room. Not a rescue. Not a speech. Just one sentence that gave her somewhere to stand.

And what was the smallest thing I could say that was true and kind at the same time? A fact, said calmly, with no accusation in it. I did not need to fix Daniel. I did not need to win an argument. I needed one true sentence, and the willingness to say it out loud.

The better move

I said, “Daniel, just to add the context, the payment API landed this morning, and Mara had the fix ready within an hour of that. The delay was the dependency, not the work.”

Then I turned to Mara. “Do you want me to take the testing, so you can close the release notes?”

Daniel paused. I had given him a fact instead of a fight, and a fact is easier to accept than a challenge. “Right,” he said. “Okay. Let’s move on.”

The meeting ended ten minutes early. Mara caught me by the coffee machine later. “Thank you,” she said. “That was the first time someone spoke for me in a room.”

And Daniel, that afternoon, was decent to Mara. Not warm, just decent. Being given a way out had let him be decent.

Why it worked for everyone

Nothing big happened that day. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But the room felt different after, the way a room does when the air changes.

Mara got her standing back. When someone speaks for you in a room, even once, you stop bracing for the next blow, and you do better work for the rest of the day. She went back to her desk lighter than she had come in.

Daniel got a way out. He did not have to double down in front of his team to save face. A fact gave him a dignified exit, and he took it, and in taking it he remembered how to be fair.

The rest of us got permission. Once one person says the true thing calmly, the next person finds it easier. Silence is contagious, but so is fairness, and it only takes one voice to start it.

And I got my evening back. I did not carry home the guilt of having watched and done nothing, the feeling that follows you to dinner and sits with you while you try to sleep. I had done a small thing, and it was enough.

Pressure needs silence to work. One sentence breaks it.

One line to carry

Say the true thing, calmly, and give them somewhere to stand.

Chapter 2: The Helpless Person

The moment

Just after eight in the morning. I was crossing the lobby with my coffee when I heard the shouting.

A visitor, a man in an expensive suit, was standing at the security desk, shouting at the guard. His badge would not work, and he was late for a meeting on the twelfth floor.

“Do you know who I am?” he said. “I have a meeting with the director. Get your manager. Now.”

The guard, Samuel, kept his voice level. “Sir, the badge system changed yesterday. I can print you a day pass in one minute. I only need your ID.”

“I don’t have time for this,” the man said, louder. “You people are useless.”

People walked past with their eyes down. A woman pretended to check her phone. A young engineer hurried for the lift. Nobody stopped.

I knew the truth. Samuel was doing his job. The badge system had changed the day before, and nobody had told the visitors. Samuel had no power in that lobby. One complaint from a man like that, and he could lose his shift. And the man was not really angry at Samuel. He was embarrassed about being late, and embarrassment, when it has nowhere to go, turns into anger at whoever cannot answer back.

And I thought the sentence again. I know, but I can do nothing. It is not my company, not my guard, not my fight. If I step in, the man turns on me, and then I am late too. Someone else will help. Samuel can handle himself.

Nobody did. The lobby kept moving around them, the way a river moves around a stone, and I stood at the edge of it with my coffee going cold.

The minute

I stopped at the edge of the lobby and gave myself one minute. One minute is not long, but it is long enough to hear yourself think.

What was actually true? The guard was right, the system had changed, and the man needed a pass, not a fight.

What would help Samuel, not in general, but in this minute? Someone standing beside him, so he was not alone in front of a shouting man.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? Treat Samuel like a person in front of the man, and give the man a way to get what he needed without losing face.

That was all. I did not need to lecture the man. I did not need to report him. I needed to stand next to Samuel and say one calm thing.

The better move

I walked over and said, “Morning, Samuel. Thanks for sorting my badge yesterday.”

Then I turned to the man. “The badges changed yesterday. Mine didn’t work either. Samuel can print you a day pass in a minute, and I’ll walk you up to twelve. You’re in the same meeting as me.”

The man’s anger had nowhere to go. I was not fighting him. Samuel was not alone anymore. And the offer was practical: a pass, a lift, a way out of the lobby without backing down.

He hesitated, then handed Samuel his ID. Samuel printed the pass. I walked the man to the lift and showed him which button to press. He muttered a thanks and went up.

Back at the desk, Samuel let out a breath. “Thank you, sir,” he said. “Nobody ever stops.”

Why it worked for everyone

Nothing big happened that morning. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But the lobby felt different after, the way a place does when someone has been kind in it.

Samuel got his dignity back. Being greeted by name and thanked, in front of the man who had just shouted at him, told him he mattered in that building. He stood a little straighter for the rest of the day, and the people who passed his desk after that got a warmer greeting than they had before.

The angry man got what he needed without having to win. He went into his meeting with a working pass instead of a story about a useless guard, and by lunchtime he had probably forgotten the lobby entirely. Embarrassment, given a way out, simply evaporates.

The people who had walked past with their eyes down saw that stopping costs one minute, and that one minute is affordable. The next time, one of them might stop.

And I got my morning back. I did not carry upstairs the guilt of having watched a man be humiliated and done nothing, the feeling that follows you to your desk and sits with you while you try to work. I had done a small thing, and it was enough.

Cruelty needs an audience that looks away. One person standing beside the helpless one changes the whole balance of the room.

One line to carry

Stand beside the person with no power, and the power in the room changes.
