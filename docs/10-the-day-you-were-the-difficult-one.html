The Minute After

Chapter 10: The Day You Were the Difficult One

The Moment

It was a Tuesday, and the room was full of senior leadership, and Mara was presenting an idea, and I realised, as she spoke, that it was an idea I had dismissed in a smaller meeting the week before.

She had raised it then, quietly, and I had said, “That won’t work,” without really listening, and the room had moved on, and she had gone quiet. And now, in front of the directors, she was presenting it properly, and it was good. It was better than good. It was the answer, and I knew it the second I heard it.

And then I did the thing.

“As I was saying last week,” I said, smoothly, without thinking, “the way to handle this is—” and I took her idea as my own, in front of the directors, in front of the room, and the room nodded, and the directors wrote it down, and for one warm second I felt the pleasure of being the one with the idea.

And then I looked at Mara, and I saw her face, and I knew, in the same second, exactly what I had done.

Her pen had stopped moving halfway down the page. Her hand was resting lightly on her laptop, as if it might steady her. She looked down at her notes, and something in me went quiet with her, because I had seen that look before. I had seen it in this same room, two weeks into this book, when Daniel had spoken over her and the room had gone quiet and I had waited too long to speak.

I had done to Mara what Daniel had done to Mara. I was the difficult one.

I know, but I can do nothing. It is already said. Correcting it now would be embarrassing. Everyone has moved on. I can credit her later, privately, and it will be just as good.

I told myself that story because it let me keep the warm feeling of being the one with the idea. It was not true. Later never comes, and private credit does not fix a public theft. Credit, taken in public, can only be returned in public. And the longer I waited, the more it would cost, because every minute I waited was a minute I was choosing the idea over the person.

The Minute

I gave myself one minute, and it was the hardest minute in this book, because it was not about someone else. It was about me.

What was actually true? The idea was hers. I had dismissed it, and she had kept it, and she had presented it better than I ever could have. That was the truth, and it was a small truth, and it did not need a defence.

What would help Mara, not in general, but right now, in this minute? Not a private word after the meeting. A public correction, in the same room, at the same volume, in front of the same people who had just watched me take it.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? Stop, mid-sentence, and say, “Actually, no. That was Mara’s idea. She raised it last week and I didn’t listen properly. Mara, tell them the rest.” I did not need to apologise for ten minutes. I did not need to explain myself. I needed one true sentence, and the willingness to be embarrassed in front of the directors, because I had earned the embarrassment.

The Better Move

I did it. I stopped, mid-sentence, and I said, “Actually, no. That was Mara’s idea. She raised it last week and I didn’t listen properly. Mara, tell them the rest.”

The room went quiet in a way I had not heard before, because this time the silence was about me. The directors looked at me, and then at Mara, and Mara looked at me, and for a second I thought I had made everything worse.

Then she spoke. She told them the rest, better than I could have, because it had always been hers, and the directors listened to her now, properly, and one of them said, “I want to hear the rest of Mara’s idea,” and she told them, and the idea got built, with her name on it.

It was awkward. Repair is awkward. The room felt the correction, and nobody pretended otherwise. But the room also saw something it does not often see: a person correcting himself, in public, at the same volume as the mistake.

After the meeting, Mara caught me by the coffee machine. “Thank you,” she said. “That cost you something.” And then, honestly, because Mara is honest now, “It took you a day.” She meant the week before, when I had dismissed it. She was right. It had taken me a week. She was gracious, but she was honest, and trust takes time, and she watched me for a while after, and I understood that. I had earned the watching.

Why It Worked for Everyone

Nothing big happened that day. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But the record was corrected, and that is not nothing.

Mara got her idea back, in the room where it mattered, in front of the people who mattered. She is still careful before she speaks, and she still checks herself, and one correction did not undo all of that. But her name is on the idea now, and the idea got built, and the next time she raises one, the room listens a little faster.

The room got a lesson nobody announced. The directors saw a correction, and that changed how they listen. One of them told me later, “I want to hear the rest of Mara’s idea,” and he meant it, and he has meant it since.

And I got something I did not expect. I got to know what it feels like to be the pressure in the room. It is easy to be decent when you are the witness. It is harder when you are the one in the wrong. I had spent nine chapters learning to speak up for other people, and the tenth chapter taught me the last lesson: the minute after is not for other people. It is for us, every time, including the days we are the difficult one.

I did the right thing, just not at first. I dismissed her idea, and then I took it, and then I gave it back, and the giving back mattered, but the taking still happened, and I carry that. I am learning to count that as a beginning, not a failure. That is what this book is, in the end. Not a book about heroes. A book about ordinary decent people, making one slightly better choice, in difficult moments, including the moments when they are the difficult one.

It is easy to be decent when you are the witness. It is harder when you are the one in the wrong.

One Line to Carry

You do not get to choose whether you are the difficult one. You get to choose what you do the minute after.
