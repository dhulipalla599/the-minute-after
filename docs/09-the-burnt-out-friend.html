The Minute After

Chapter 9: The Burnt-Out Friend

The Moment

It was Daniel again, but this time I saw him properly, and I wished I had seen him sooner.

Three weeks after the incident review. Daniel stayed late every night, and he ate lunch at his desk, and his replies got shorter, and his jacket hung over his chair for days without moving. His eyes had the look of a person who has not slept properly in a while, and he rubbed the back of his neck the way people do when the tension has settled there and will not leave.

And then, in standup, he snapped at Lena. It was small, and it was sharp, and he regretted it the second it left his mouth. I saw the regret cross his face, the way a cloud crosses the sun, and I saw Lena go quiet, and I saw the room go quiet with her.

I noticed, and I did nothing. For two weeks I noticed and did nothing.

I know, but I can do nothing. He is the lead. He is an adult. He will ask if he needs help. It is not my place.

I told myself that story because it let me keep walking past his desk. It was not true. Burnout does not announce itself. It just gets quieter, and then it snaps. And the people who need help most are the ones least likely to ask for it, because asking feels like admitting they cannot carry it, and Daniel had spent months carrying it, the client, the dates, the release, the incident, all of it, and he was running on empty and pretending otherwise, and everyone noticed, and no one said anything, and I was no one.

The Minute

I stood at the end of the row of desks, after standup, and I gave myself one minute.

What was actually true? Daniel was exhausted, and the work would not stop asking. One afternoon off would not fix it, and I knew that. But one afternoon off was a start, and a start was better than watching.

What would help him, not in general, but right now, in this minute? Not advice. Not a lecture about work-life balance, which is the last thing a burnt-out person needs to hear from someone who is not burnt out. A question, asked without an agenda, when no one else was listening.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? “Daniel. How are you, really?” And then, whatever he said, an offer with no strings: “Let me take your standup tomorrow. Go home at five. The release will survive.”

The Better Move

I waited until the room had emptied, and I walked over to his desk, and I said, “Daniel. How are you, really?”

He deflected, the way people deflect when they have been deflecting for weeks. “Fine. Busy. You know how it is.” He tried to smile, and the smile did not reach his eyes.

I did not push. I just said, “I’m taking your standup tomorrow. Go home at five. The release will survive.”

He resisted, the way people resist when someone offers them the one thing they need and they do not know how to accept it. “I can’t. There’s the—” and then he stopped, and he went quiet, and he nodded, once, the way a person nods when they have just been given permission to do the thing they have been needing to do for weeks.

He left at five. The first time in weeks. I took his standup the next morning, and it was fine. Nothing broke. The release survived, which is what releases do when one person goes home on time.

Why It Worked for Everyone

Nothing big happened that week. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But Daniel went home at five, and that is not nothing.

Daniel got an afternoon, and an afternoon is not a cure. He still had the same job, and the same pressure, and the same boss who wanted dates. But he slept, and he came back the next day a little more human, and a week later, over coffee, he told me, quietly, as a fact and not a drama, that he had been close to quitting. He did not say it to be thanked. He said it because the door had stayed open, and he could walk through it now.

Lena got an apology. Daniel went to her the next day, briefly, awkwardly, the way real apologies are, and he said he had been wrong to snap at her, and she said it was all right, and it was not quite all right, but it was a start. She learned that adults can repair things, and that is a lesson worth more than any document.

The team got a door that stayed open. The next time someone stayed late three nights in a row, someone else asked how they were, really, and nobody was surprised. Noticing is not helping. It is only watching with better information.

And I got my evening back, mostly. I did not carry home the picture of a friend running on empty while I watched from my desk, telling myself it was not my place. But I waited two weeks too long. I noticed weeks ago, and noticing is not helping. I am learning that the minute after is sometimes the minute after you have already waited too long, and it is still worth taking.

The people who need help most are the ones least likely to ask for it.

One Line to Carry

How are you, really? Ask it like you mean it.
