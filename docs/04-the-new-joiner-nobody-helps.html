The Minute After

Chapter 4: The New Joiner Nobody Helps

The Moment

Monday morning. Her name was Lena, and she was standing by the coffee machine holding a laptop and a badge that still had the new plastic shine on it, looking at the floor plan on the wall as if it might explain everything.

It was her first week. Nobody had shown her where anything was. Not the build, not the on-call rota, not who to ask, not how things really worked here. The wiki was out of date, and the real knowledge lived in people’s heads, which is the worst place to keep it if you are new. Everyone was too busy to notice. I was too busy to notice. I walked past her on my way to standup, and I told myself someone else would help. Someone always helps the new person. That is what I told myself.

At standup she asked a question, and someone answered it in jargon nobody explained, and she nodded the way people nod when they have stopped understanding but do not want to say so. Her laptop stayed close to her chest, like a shield. At lunch she sat alone, reading the wiki on her phone, eating a sandwich one-handed.

I walked past her a second time that afternoon, and this time I could not pretend I had not seen her. I know, but I can do nothing. I have my own deadlines. The migration is behind. Someone else will help. She will figure it out, the way we all did.

I told myself that story because it let me keep walking. It was not true, or not the whole truth. She would figure it out, eventually, the way we all did, slowly and alone and at three times the cost. And the reason I walked past was not that I was busy. The reason was that nobody had helped me in my first week either, and I had come to think of that as normal, instead of what it was: a small unkindness, repeated so often it looked like a process.

The Minute

I stopped at the end of the corridor and gave myself one minute.

What was actually true? She was lost, and ten minutes of my time would save her ten hours. That was not generosity. It was arithmetic.

What would help her, not in general, but right now, in this minute? Not a document. A person. A name. A map of who knows what, and permission to ask.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? Walk over, sit beside her, and say, “Come and sit with me for ten minutes. I will show you the build, and I will introduce you to Mara.” Ten minutes. I had ten minutes. I had spent longer than that deciding whether to help.

The Better Move

I went over and said it. “Lena, right? Come and sit with me for ten minutes. I will show you the build, and I will introduce you to Mara. Mara knows the payment service better than anyone.”

She looked relieved and embarrassed at the same time, the way people do when help arrives and they are not sure they deserved it. We sat down, and I showed her the build, and the deploy, and the on-call rota, and I told her the things the wiki does not say. Who to ask for what. That Daniel likes dates on the board. That the wiki lies about the on-call rota. That the payment service is the one service you do not touch on a Friday.

Ten minutes became twenty. She asked a question I was too proud to admit I had also asked in my first month, and I answered it honestly, and something in her shoulders came down, the way a person’s shoulders come down when they realise they are not the only one who did not know.

Then I took her to Mara, and Mara, who remembered what it felt like to be spoken over in a room, made space for her without making a fuss of it. “Ask me anything,” Mara said. “I mean it. I was new once too.”

Why It Worked for Everyone

Nothing big happened that day. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But Lena’s first month went a different way, and that is not nothing.

Lena got two people to ask, and that was enough. Her first month was still hard. She still got lost, and she still had a bug that took her three days, and she still ate lunch alone sometimes. But she stopped eating lunch alone every day. By the end of the month she answered someone else’s question in standup, in plain words, and I watched her do it and thought, that is how it spreads.

Mara got something back too. Being the person who knows, being asked, gave her a standing she had been rebuilding since that meeting two weeks ago. Teaching is a quiet way of belonging, and she belonged a little more after that.

The team got a habit nobody had to announce. The next new joiner got a buddy without anyone being asked, because Lena did it, without thinking about it, the way people do things they were once grateful for.

And I got my evening back, mostly. I did not carry home the picture of a new person eating alone while I walked past with my deadlines. But I still wish I had stopped the first time, not the third. I did the right thing, just not at first. Nobody remembers their first week clearly, which is why nobody helps with it. I am trying to remember mine.

Ten minutes of a person’s time can save them ten hours of being lost.

One Line to Carry

Everyone remembers the one person who stopped.
