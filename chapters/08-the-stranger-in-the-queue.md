# Chapter 8: The Stranger in the Queue

## The Moment

Saturday morning, the grocery store. I was standing in the queue with a cart full of milk and bread and the week's ordinary things, and I was in a hurry, and the woman ahead of me was slow.

She was old, older than my mother would be, with a coat too warm for the day and a small purse with a clasp, and she was counting coins on the counter, one at a time, her hands trembling a little. The cashier waited. The queue waited. And the queue sighed.

I sighed too.

It was a small sound, and I did not mean it to be unkind, but a sigh in a queue costs nothing to the person who sighs it, and everything to the person it lands on. The woman heard it. I saw her shoulders come up, the way a person's shoulders come up when they are embarrassed in front of strangers, and she said, to no one in particular, "Sorry, sorry, I'm nearly there."

The man behind me muttered, "Come on." The cashier's patience was wearing thin. And the woman kept counting, her hands shaking a little more now, because being watched makes your hands shake more, and being rushed makes them shake more still.

I know, but I can do nothing. I am in a hurry too. Someone else will help. She will manage. It is not my place to interfere with a stranger's shopping.

I told myself that story because it let me keep sighing. It was not true. She was not slow on purpose. She was doing her best with what she had, in front of strangers, and she was embarrassed, and embarrassment, when it has nowhere to go, turns into shame, and shame makes your hands shake. And she reminded me of my mother, counting change at a market a long time ago, careful with every coin, because every coin mattered, and I had forgotten that until I saw her hands.

## The Minute

I stood in the queue with my cart, and I gave myself one minute.

What was actually true? She was not slow. She was careful. And she was one small amount short, or nearly short, and the queue was making it harder, not easier. That was the truth, and it was a small truth, and it did not need a speech.

What would help her, not in general, but right now, in this minute? Not charity, announced loudly, in front of everyone. A quiet hand. Pay the difference, or help her pack, or simply stand with her, so she was not alone in front of the sighs.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? Step forward, quietly, and say, "I've got the rest." No fuss. No speech. Just a few coins, and a pair of hands to help with the bags.

## The Better Move

I stepped forward and said, quietly, to the cashier, "I've got the rest."

It was a few dollars. I put them on the counter, and I helped the woman put her bags in her cart, and I did not make a speech about it, because kindness, done quietly, does not ask to be noticed. It just asks to be enough.

She looked at me, embarrassed and grateful at the same time, the way people are when help arrives and they are not sure they deserved it. "You're very kind," she said.

"It was nothing," I said, which was not quite true, but it was the kind thing to say, and sometimes the kind thing is not the exact thing.

The man behind me said nothing. The queue moved on. The cashier relaxed, and smiled at the next customer a little more easily, and the woman went out with her groceries and her dignity, and that is enough to hope for.

## Why It Worked for Everyone

Nothing big happened that morning. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But a stranger's morning went a different way, and that is not nothing.

The woman got her groceries and her dignity, and she left with both. She still has a hard week ahead, and a few dollars did not change that. But that morning was a little lighter, and a morning is not nothing.

The cashier got her patience back. She had been waiting too, and waiting while a queue sighs is its own kind of hard. She relaxed, and the next customer got a warmer greeting than they would have, and that is how it spreads.

The queue got a lesson nobody announced. The sighing man did not change. He just moved on, and he will sigh again next week. But the person behind me saw it, and maybe they will remember, and maybe the next time they are in a queue behind someone slow, they will think of the woman with the purse, and they will wait.

And I got my morning back, mostly. I did not carry home the picture of an old woman counting coins while I stood behind her, sighing, glad to be in a hurry. My first instinct was to be in a hurry. My second was to be a person. I am glad the second one won, and I wish it had not needed a fight.

Patience is not waiting quietly. It is deciding the stranger's dignity is worth your minute.

## One Line to Carry

The queue can wait. A person's dignity cannot.
