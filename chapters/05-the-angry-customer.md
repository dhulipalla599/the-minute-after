# Chapter 5: The Angry Customer

## The Moment

Tuesday, late afternoon. I was walking back from the coffee machine when I heard the shouting, and it was coming through the speaker on Lena's desk, loud enough for the whole open office to hear.

Lena was three weeks into her job, and she was on the support rotation, and a client was shouting at her about the outage that morning. His name was Mr. Alvarez, and his voice filled the room.

"This is unacceptable. Do you understand? Unacceptable. My customers have been waiting since nine o'clock. Get me someone who actually knows what they are doing."

Lena kept saying, "I understand, sir," while he talked over her, and her hands were shaking under the desk where she thought nobody could see them. She had the fix in front of her. She knew what had happened. But she was twenty-three, and three weeks into a job, and a man was shouting at her in front of an office full of people, and knowing the answer is not the same as being able to say it while someone shouts.

I know, but I can do nothing. It is not my call. It is not my client. Someone senior will step in. And then the thought I am not proud of, the one that came before all of those: thank goodness it is not me.

I stood there with my coffee going cold in my hand, and I was glad it was not me, and a small, ugly part of me was even glad to be watching it happen to someone else, because watching is easier than being in it. I am not proud of that. But it is true, and I have learned not to trust the first feeling. The first feeling is usually the one that is trying to keep you safe. The second one is usually the one trying to keep you decent.

And I had already decided who the villain was. The man on the speaker, obviously, a bully with a broken service. I was wrong about that too. He was not really angry at Lena. He was embarrassed. His own customers were waiting, his boss was standing behind him, he had promised them it would work, and he was afraid of walking into his own meeting looking like a man who could not deliver. A frightened person shouts at the nearest voice that cannot shout back. Lena was the nearest voice.

## The Minute

I stood at the end of the row of desks and gave myself one minute.

What was actually true? The outage was fixed. What he needed now was not another apology. He needed a straight answer, a time, and a person. That was the truth, and it was a small truth, the kind that fits in one sentence.

What would help Lena, not in general, but right now, in this minute? Someone beside her, taking the weight of the call, so she was not alone in front of a shouting man and an office full of listeners.

What was the smallest thing I could do that was true and decent at the same time? Pull up a chair, sit beside her, and take the call with her. No lecture. No rescue. Just a second voice, and a plain answer.

## The Better Move

I put my coffee down, pulled up a chair, and said, quietly, "Lena, let me sit in on this one."

Then I leaned toward the speaker. "Mr. Alvarez, this is the engineer who worked on the fix. I am sitting with Lena now. The outage was a failed deploy at nine this morning. It is fixed, and your service has been up since eleven. I will send you the incident note within the hour, and this is my direct number if anything looks wrong."

He stopped shouting. Anger has nowhere to go when nobody fights back. I had not argued with him. I had not apologised five times. I had given him a fact, a time, and a name, and that was what he had actually wanted, underneath the shouting.

"Fine," he said, quieter now. "Send the note. And thank you." At the end, stiffly, to Lena: "Thank you for your time." It was not warm. It was stiff. But it was something, and she nodded, and her hands stopped shaking.

## Why It Worked for Everyone

Nothing big happened that afternoon. No one was punished, no policy was written, no one gave a speech. But the call ended, and the office went back to work, and that is not nothing.

Lena got her dignity back, and she got a lesson nobody can teach in a document. She saw what a hard call sounds like when someone experienced is sitting beside you, and she saw that calm is not the absence of feeling. She still shook after, and she still went for a walk around the block, and she still came back steadier than she left. The next time a customer raised his voice, she did not go pale first. She went quiet, and then she answered.

Mr. Alvarez got what he needed without having to win. He went into his meeting with a straight answer and a person to call, instead of a story about a useless support desk. He did not become a friend. He called back the next day and asked for me by name, and that is enough to hope for. His own customers never knew about the shouting. They only knew the service was back.

The office got a lesson too. Everyone who heard that call learned what calm sounds like, and one of them told me later that they would have frozen too, and that they were glad someone sat down. Calm is not the absence of feeling. It is the decision not to pass it on.

And I got my evening back, mostly. I did not carry home the picture of a young woman shaking at her desk while I stood there with a cold coffee, glad it was not me. My first instinct was to be glad it wasn't me. My second instinct was better, and I am learning to trust the second one.

Anger is usually fear with the volume turned up.

## One Line to Carry

You cannot argue a frightened person calm. You can only be calm first.
